\subsection{Data Processing}

Many of the pre-trained models that we have incorporated into the project experiments were trained on data that had certain requirements. For example, most models were trained on audio data sampled at a rate of 16kHz, but some audio has higher resolution and is recorded at 48kHz.

As the models were trained on 16kHz data, providing them with 48kHz data would not be optimal. It is, however, possible to downsample signals between two different rates with ease\footnote{as long as ratio is a natural number}. Assume in our case, we want to go from 48kHz to 16kHz;  $\frac{48}{16} - 1 = \frac{3}{1} - 1 = 2$ zeros between every point in the original signal. A \textit{low pass filter} is then applied on half of lowest frequency of the two (8kHz). This ensures no aliasing occurs when we downsample the signal. In the example we then sample every 3rd point in the now filtered signal.

CoRal dataset is recoreded in 48kHz and we downsample it to 16kHz; Librispeech is sampled at 16k and no downsampling is required.

\subsubsection{Data Augmentation}

When working with any kind of data, for most machine learning tasks or models, the more data available the better. The more complex and diverse a true data distribution is, the more data is generally required for sufficient models to learn the patterns and distributions in the data. However, labeling and collecting data is of course expensive, as it requires humans 'experts' to sit down, collect and annotate the data. In the domain of audio, this can be quite expensive, as transcription of audio is a time consuming menial task.

In such cases, data augmentation is a common technique to artificially increase the amount of data available to train models. The augmentations usually rely on simple and easy transformations of training data, such that humans would easily remark that it is a duplicate sample. However, such transformations have proven in some domains to be quite beneficial. For example, in vision domains, augmentations such as flipping, cropping, rotation and noise diffusion can all help models gain a better generalization of the data.\cite{perez_effectiveness_2017}

In regards to audio, augmentations exists as well. Augmentations here can help alleviate if the data is distributed around too few speakers. It is easy to convince oneself, for example, if that the audio one is training on, is from male speakers, that it would have a hard time identifying speech from female speakers. Even moreso, as different people speak with different accents, dialects and pronunciations. Data augmentation can possibly alleviate the problem to a certain degree.

In order to see if augmentations can help one of our models in any capacity, we will implement three augmentations and try them out individually. They are \textit{amplitude noise diffusion}, \textit{band stop filtering}, and \textit{pitch shifting}. These techniques were explored in a recent paper by. \cite{anidjar_whisper_2024} In this paper, the authors explore the three augmentations, and combinations of them, to see how it help the Whisper model perform better on low-resource languages, particularly portuguese and arabic. They find that the results are quite promising. They furthermore only work with few speakers and few hours of training data.

The dataset we work with is not as "low-resourced" (andet ord) than the one they explore in the paper. They also have fewer speakers as part of the data they train on, so expected is that data augmentation will not help as much in our case, as it does in their case.

\textbf{Amplitude Noise}

One very simple idea is to add noise to the audio signal in question. The noise can be sampled in different ways, usually it is either gaussian or uniformly distributed though. In the case of a normal distribution, the noise is sampled from one with a mean of 0, and a variance equal to the level of noise one wishes to add. In the paper, the authors applied a uniform distribution instead, ranging from 0.001 to 0.05. The noise is then added to the original signal after multiplying it.

$$ X_{new} = X_{original} + X_{original} \cdot U(\text{low}, \text{high}) $$

Note, the noise is sampled at a point basis; i.e., the noise at point $i$ is independent of the noise at point $j$. The probability of adding noise to a sample can be set, and from the paper we set it to 20\% of the time. As such, after 5 epochs, all training data have been sampled at least once with noise on average.

\textbf{Band Stop Filtering}

Band stop filtering is a technique used in signal processing to filter out a range of frequencies from a signal. This can be a more destructive augmentation techniques than adding noise, as it can remove the frequencies that are important for the underlying speech signal.
